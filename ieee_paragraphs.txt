006: I.ABSRACT
007: Psychological stress is a critical public health challenge underlying cardiovascular disease, depression, and burnout. Objective, real-time stress monitoring through wearable Electrocardiogram sensors can enable timely clinical intervention. This paper proposes an end-to-end deep learning pipeline that classifies ECG segments as Stress or No-Stress without handcrafted feature engineering. The architecture stacks two Conv1D layers for local waveform feature extraction, two Bidirectional LSTM (BiLSTM) layers for forward-and-backward temporal dependency modelling, and a custom Attention mechanism to selectively amplify stress-discriminative heartbeat segments. Convolutional layers are pre-trained on the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia and DREAMER datasets before fine-tuning on WESAD. Preprocessing combines Butterworth bandpass filtering, Pan-Tompkins R-peak detection, sliding-window segmentation, Z-score normalization, SMOTE balancing, and data augmentation. Target metrics are: accuracy ≥ 92%, sensitivity ≥ 94%, specificity ≥ 90%, and AUC-ROC ≥ 0.95. Societal value includes enabling low-cost wearable stress screening for workplace wellness programs, remote patient monitoring, and early preventive cardiovascular care.
008: Index Terms—ECG, stress detection, convolutional neural network, bidirectional LSTM, attention mechanism, WESAD, MIT-BIH, transfer learning, SMOTE, deep learning, wearable health monitoring.
009: II. INTRODUCTION
010: Workplace stress, exam anxiety, and chronic psychosocial pressure have emerged as primary contributors to the global burden of non-communicable diseases. The World Health Organization estimates that stress-related mental health disorders cost the global economy over one trillion US dollars annually in lost productivity, absenteeism, and healthcare expenditure [11]. Despite this scale, current clinical assessment relies almost entirely on subjective instruments — the Perceived Stress Scale, Spielberger State-Trait Anxiety Inventory, and clinical interviews — all of which are susceptible to recall bias, demand characteristics, and insufficient temporal resolution to track within-day stress dynamics.
011: The autonomic nervous system encodes the physiological stress response in measurable cardiovascular signals. Sympathetic activation during acute stress elevates heart rate, shortens RR intervals, and suppresses high-frequency heart rate variability (HRV) components. These changes are faithfully reflected in the Electrocardiogram, which can be continuously acquired via affordable, non-invasive, wearable chest-band or patch sensors. ECG therefore occupies a privileged position among candidate bio-signals for unobtrusive stress monitoring: it is physiologically informative, measurement-mature, and increasingly accessible via consumer wearables.
012: Classical computational approaches to ECG stress detection decompose the problem into two sequential phases: (1) manual feature engineering, computing time-domain HRV statistics (SDNN, RMSSD, pNN50), frequency-domain spectral ratios (LF/HF power), and nonlinear entropy indices; (2) supervised classification using support vector machines or shallow ensemble methods. These pipelines require substantial domain expertise, are brittle to signal quality variation, and fail to leverage the full information content of raw ECG waveforms. Deep learning sidesteps manual feature design entirely, learning hierarchical task-specific representations directly from raw signals.
013: This paper presents a hybrid CNN-BiLSTM + Attention architecture that unifies convolutional local pattern recognition, bidirectional temporal sequence modeling, and learnable attention weighting in an end-to-end framework for binary ECG stress classification. Transfer learning from MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database [13] and the DREAMER affective dataset pre-initializes convolutional weights with clinically robust cardiac feature representations. SMOTE oversampling and multi-strategy data augmentation address the inherent class imbalance and distribution shift challenges of physiological datasets.
014: The key contributions are:
015: (1) A five-stage preprocessing chain (bandpass filtering, R-peak detection, windowing, normalization, SMOTE balancing) with four-strategy data augmentation.
016: (2) A CNN-BiLSTM + Attention model exploiting transfer learning from MIT-BIH and DREAMER for feature initialization.
017: (3) Rigorous evaluation against clinical performance targets (sensitivity ≥ 94%, specificity ≥ 90%, AUC ≥ 0.95) on the WESAD benchmark.
018: (4) A deployable Streamlit web application for real-time ECG stress inference.

033: IV. BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK
034: A. Physiological Basis of ECG Stress Markers
035: The cardiac stress response is mediated by the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and the sympatho-adrenal medullary system, collectively elevating circulating catecholamines that increase heart rate and reduce parasympathetic tone. In frequency-domain HRV analysis, acute stress suppresses the high-frequency (HF) band (0.15–0.40 Hz)  a marker of respiratory sinus arrhythmia and vagal modulation  while increasing the low-frequency (LF) to HF power ratio, an index of sympathovagal balance [2]. These autonomic changes manifest in ECG morphology as shortened RR intervals, reduced inter-beat interval variability, and subtle waveform amplitude modifications detectable by learned deep representations.
036: B. Classical Machine Learning for Stress Detection
037: Healey and Picard [2] pioneered wearable physiological stress detection, demonstrating that ECG and galvanic skin response features measured during real-world driving could classify three levels of cognitive-emotional load using linear discriminant analysis. Schmidt et al. [1] subsequently introduced the WESAD dataset and established baseline performance using LDA applied to HRV features, achieving 80.3% accuracy under leave-one-subject-out cross-validation. Support vector machine classifiers extending the WESAD HRV feature set have approached 83–86% accuracy but exhibit sensitivity to signal artifacts and cross-subject physiological variability.
038: C. Deep Learning Approaches
039: Rajpurkar et al. [3] demonstrated that a 34-layer 1-D residual convolutional network trained on a large single-lead ECG corpus could match cardiologist performance in arrhythmia classification, establishing the viability of raw-waveform deep learning for cardiac signal interpretation. For stress recognition, Sarkar and Etemad [4] proposed self-supervised pre-training of CNN encoders on unlabeled ECG data followed by task-specific fine-tuning, demonstrating that contrastive representation learning improves stress classification in low-label regimes. Nath et al. [5] combined LSTM temporal modeling with HRV features, achieving 88.4% accuracy on WESAD. Bobade and Vani [6] employed CNN-LSTM fusion across multiple physiological modalities to reach 90.1%.
040: D. Bidirectional LSTM and Attention
041: Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory networks [12], [15] extend standard LSTM by concurrently processing input sequences in both temporal directions, producing hidden states that integrate both past and future context at every time step. This bidirectionality is particularly advantageous for ECG stress classification: stress-related changes in RR interval dynamics are most discriminative when evaluated within their sequential context across multiple preceding and succeeding cardiac cycles. Attention mechanisms [7], initially introduced for neural machine translation sequence alignment, apply learnable salience weights to intermediate sequence representations, enabling the network to focus computation on the most diagnostically informative heartbeat segments. In physiological signal processing, attention maps additionally provide post-hoc interpretability for clinician inspection.
042: E. Transfer Learning in Biomedical Signal Processing
043: Transfer learning from large, annotation-rich biomedical datasets to smaller task-specific corpora is an established practice for improving deep learning performance in data-scarce medical AI applications. The MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database [13], containing 48 annotated half-hour ECG recordings from 47 subjects across multiple arrhythmia classes, provides a rich pre-training source for convolutional ECG feature extractors. Convolutional layers pre-trained on MIT-BIH learn generalizable cardiac waveform representations — QRS complex detection, P-wave and T-wave localization, beat morphology encoding — that transfer effectively to downstream stress classification tasks. The DREAMER affective dataset [16], containing ECG recordings during emotional stimulation across valence and arousal dimensions, contributes emotion-relevant autonomic feature priors.
044: V. PROPOSED METHODOLOGY
045: A. Datasets
046: Three publicly available datasets were utilized, each serving a distinct role in the experimental pipeline.
047: TABLE I: Datasets Used in the Proposed System
048: WESAD [1] is the primary experimental benchmark. Chest-worn ECG recorded at 700 Hz via the RespiBAN sensor provides the input signal. Following established protocol [1], label category 1 (neutral baseline) and label category 2 (TSST-induced acute stress) are retained as binary classification targets; transient and amusement conditions are excluded. The corpus encompasses approximately 490 minutes of labeled ECG from 15 participants with an inherent class imbalance of 63.9% no-stress versus 36.1% stress. MIT-BIH [13] serves as the transfer learning source for convolutional pre-training; its annotation richness across diverse arrhythmia morphologies ensures that pre-trained filters encode clinically generalizable cardiac waveform primitives. DREAMER [16] provides affective physiological feature priors that complement WESAD's stress-specific supervision.
049: B. Preprocessing Pipeline
050: Stage 1 — Bandpass Filtering: A 4th-order zero-phase Butterworth bandpass filter with passband 0.5–40 Hz suppresses baseline wander (below 0.5 Hz) and powerline interference (50/60 Hz) while preserving all clinically relevant ECG components: P-waves (0.5–10 Hz), QRS complexes (5–40 Hz), and T-waves (1–7 Hz).
051: Stage 2 — R-Peak Detection: The Pan-Tompkins algorithm [14] locates R-peaks via successive differentiation, squaring, and moving-window integration of the bandpass-filtered signal, enabling accurate RR-interval extraction. RR intervals supplement the raw waveform as an additional feature channel and provide the basis for HRV-based data quality assessment.
052: Stage 3 — Sliding Window Segmentation: The signal is partitioned into 250-sample windows (≈357 ms at 700 Hz) with 50% temporal overlap, capturing 2–3 complete cardiac cycles per segment. Window-level labels are assigned by majority vote.
053: Stage 4 — Z-Score Normalization: Each window is independently standardized to zero mean and unit standard deviation, removing inter-subject amplitude offsets while preserving within-window morphological information. A stability constant ε = 10⁻⁸ prevents division by near-zero standard deviations on artifact-contaminated segments.
054: Stage 5 — SMOTE Balancing: The Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique [9] generates synthetic minority-class (stress) ECG window instances via k-nearest-neighbour interpolation in feature space, restoring class parity without discarding majority-class samples and without the information loss of random under sampling.

056: C. Data Augmentation
057: Four augmentation strategies are applied stochastically during training to improve robustness to real-world signal variability and reduce overfitting: (1) Gaussian Noise Injection — additive white Gaussian noise at SNR 30–40 dB simulates sensor noise and electrode contact variation; (2) Time Stretching — compression and expansion of window length by ±5% followed by resampling to 250 samples simulates heart rate fluctuations; (3) Amplitude Scaling — random scaling of signal amplitude within ±10% models inter-session impedance variation; (4) Random Time Shifting — shifting the window start position by ±12 samples introduces cardiac cycle phase invariance.
058: D. Transfer Learning Protocol
059: Convolutional layers are pre-trained on the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database in a multi-class beat classification task (Normal, Left Bundle Branch Block, Right Bundle Branch Block, Premature Ventricular Contraction). Pre-training employs the same two-layer Conv1D architecture (64→128 filters) for 20 epochs on 90,000 annotated beat segments. Frozen pre-trained weights are loaded as initialization for WESAD fine-tuning, with the conv layers unfrozen after 5 warm-up epochs to allow task-specific adaptation. DREAMER affective features are incorporated as auxiliary supervision during early fine-tuning epochs to embed emotional arousal priors. This protocol follows established transfer learning practices for biomedical signal classification.
060: E. Model Architecture
061: The CNN-BiLSTM + Attention model accepts ECG windows of shape (250, 1) and processes them through four hierarchical stages.
062: Conv1D Feature Extraction: Two stacked Conv1D layers (64 filters, kernel 5, ReLU; 128 filters, kernel 5, ReLU) extract progressively abstract local morphological features, each followed by Batch Normalization [17] to stabilize internal feature distributions. MaxPooling1D (pool 2) reduces temporal dimension to 125 and introduces translational invariance; Dropout (0.3) follows.
063: Bidirectional LSTM: Two stacked BiLSTM layers (128 units per direction; 64 units per direction) model long-range sequential dependencies in both temporal directions. BiLSTM was selected over BiGRU because LSTM's dual-gate (input + forget) memory architecture provides superior retention of long-horizon cardiac cycle context, which spans multiple seconds in lower-stress states. Recurrent Dropout (0.2) and input Dropout (0.2) are applied. Return sequences are enabled to pass the full temporal output to the attention layer.
064: Attention Mechanism: A Dense(1) + Tanh projection followed by Sigmoid normalization computes per-timestep salience weights in the range (0, 1). Sigmoid — not Softmax — is employed to avoid degenerate all-ones weighting when applied to a unit-dimensional axis. Weights are multiplied element-wise with BiLSTM outputs; GlobalAveragePooling1D aggregates the weighted sequence to a fixed-length feature vector.
065: Classification Head: Dense(64) + ReLU + L2 + Dropout(0.4); Dense(32) + ReLU + L2 + Dropout(0.3); Dense(1) + Sigmoid. Binary cross-entropy loss; Adam optimizer [10] with initial lr = 0.001; class weights to further address residual imbalance.

067: F. Training Configuration

073: The pipeline is partitioned into three operational zones. The Preprocessing Zone (Stages 1–5) transforms raw continuous ECG into normalized, class-balanced, fixed-length windows ready for model ingestion. The Transfer Learning Zone initializes convolutional weights with cardiac feature priors from MIT-BIH and DREAMER, enabling convergence on the limited 15-subject WESAD corpus. The Inference Zone executes the trained CNN-BiLSTM + Attention forward pass and delivers a binary stress verdict with an associated attention saliency map for clinical interpretability.
074: VII. EVALUATION STRATEGY
075: A. Performance Metrics
076: The evaluation protocol employs six clinically motivated metrics selected to capture complementary aspects of binary classifier performance on imbalanced physiological datasets.
077: TABLE V: Target Evaluation Metrics and Clinical Rationale
078: Sensitivity is designated the primary clinical metric. In healthcare stress monitoring deployment, failure to detect a genuine stress event (false negative) carries greater clinical cost than generating a false alarm (false positive): a missed stress episode in a patient with cardiovascular risk factors could delay critical intervention. The sensitivity target of ≥ 94% was therefore set above all other metric thresholds, reflecting this asymmetric misclassification cost.
079: B. Generalization Protocol
080: A person-independent evaluation protocol is enforced: the test partition is constructed to contain windows exclusively from subjects absent from the training and validation partitions. This subject-disjoint split constitutes a more stringent generalization test than random window-level splitting, as it requires the model to classify ECG patterns from physiologically distinct individuals not encountered during optimization. All reported metrics reflect this more challenging evaluation setting. Additionally, an overfitting diagnostic compares training, validation, and test accuracy and AUC-ROC: discrepancies exceeding 5% in accuracy or 3% in AUC trigger additional regularization review.
081: C. Confidence and Interpretability Analysis
082: A prediction confidence audit examines the distribution of model output probabilities on the validation partition. Predictions are categorized into high-confidence no-stress (probability < 0.30), uncertain (0.30–0.70), and high-confidence stress (probability > 0.70). Models with more than 10% of predictions in the uncertain band are flagged for architectural review, as high clinical utility requires decisive probability assignments. Attention weight maps are additionally visualized as overlays on ECG waveforms, enabling post-hoc inspection of which heartbeat segments the model identified as most informative for each prediction — a requirement for explainable AI in clinical settings.
083: VIII. CLINICAL WEB APPLICATION
084: A browser-based real-time inference interface was developed using Streamlit v1.32.0 to facilitate clinical deployment without requiring specialized programming skills. The application is architected as a three-tier system: a TensorFlow model backend, a NumPy/Pandas data processing middleware, and an interactive Streamlit frontend.
085: Two input modalities are supported. In CSV Upload mode, users submit raw ECG sample values recorded at 700 Hz; the application automatically applies the complete preprocessing chain, executes window-level inference, and aggregates predictions into a holistic stress assessment. In Demo Mode, configurable synthetic ECG signals (Normal, Moderate, Stressed) enable exploratory evaluation without requiring real physiological recordings.
086: Output visualization includes: (1) a binary stress/no-stress verdict card with confidence percentage; (2) an interactive ECG waveform plot with synchronized per-window stress probability bars; (3) attention weight overlay highlighting which cardiac cycles most influenced the prediction; and (4) personalized clinical recommendations calibrated to predicted stress level and model confidence. Inference latency for a 30-second recording is under one second, meeting clinical workflow responsiveness requirements. The attention visualization constitutes the primary explainability interface, enabling clinicians to verify that model decisions are grounded in physiologically plausible cardiac features rather than dataset artifacts.
087: IX. SOCIETAL VALUE AND IMPACT
088: The proposed system addresses a substantial unmet need in public health infrastructure. Workplace stress costs employers an estimated $300 billion annually in the United States alone through healthcare expenditure, turnover, and productivity loss. Objective, continuous, wearable stress monitoring can enable three high-impact application pathways.
089: Preventive Cardiovascular Care: Chronic stress is an independent risk factor for hypertension, atherosclerosis, and myocardial infarction. Automated ECG-based stress alerts integrated into wearable devices could trigger early clinical referral for at-risk individuals before cardiovascular events occur, shifting care from reactive to preventive.
090: Workplace Wellness Programs: Organizations deploying wearable ECG stress monitors could use anonymized population-level stress analytics to identify high-burden work conditions, evaluate intervention effectiveness, and customize workload distribution — translating physiological data into organizational health management decisions.
091: Remote Patient Monitoring: In low-resource and rural healthcare settings where clinical psychologist access is limited, ECG-based stress detection provides an objective, low-cost alternative to in-person psychological assessment. Integration with telemedicine platforms could democratize stress-related mental health screening at population scale, particularly relevant in post-pandemic healthcare infrastructure rebuilding.
092: Educational Performance Monitoring: Stress detection during examination periods could enable adaptive learning platforms to adjust content difficulty, pacing, and intervention delivery based on detected physiological stress levels, improving educational outcomes for students with stress-related learning difficulties.
093: X. DISCUSSION
094: A. Architectural Choices
095: Three design decisions differentiate the proposed approach from the prior CNN-BiGRU and LSTM literature. First, BiLSTM was selected over BiGRU based on the task-specific advantage of LSTM's dual-gate memory architecture for retaining cardiac context across the extended inter-beat interval sequences (up to 125 time steps post-pooling) required for reliable HRV-based stress discrimination. Second, Sigmoid normalization in the attention layer prevents the degenerate all-ones output produced by Softmax when applied to a unit-dimensional axis. Third, Global Average Pooling following attention aggregation enforces temporal position invariance, preventing the model from exploiting window-position artifacts that arise from the fixed-offset sliding window segmentation scheme.
096: B. Transfer Learning Benefit
097: Pre-training on MIT-BIH addresses the fundamental data scarcity challenge of the WESAD corpus: only 15 subjects are available for training, validation, and test, severely limiting the number of independent physiological examples that can be presented to the network. Transfer learning effectively supplements this dataset by initializing convolutional filters with waveform representations learned from 48 subjects across diverse cardiac morphologies, providing a substantially richer initialization than random weight assignment. The pre-training benefit is particularly pronounced for low-data fine-tuning scenarios, where randomly initialized models tend to overfit to subject-specific idiosyncrasies in the small WESAD training set.
098: C. Limitations
099: The study carries several limitations. WESAD subjects are demographically restricted to young healthy adults, limiting generalizability to populations with cardiac comorbidities, older age groups, and clinical patient cohorts. The 250-sample window length optimized for morphological classification may be insufficient for long-horizon HRV spectral analysis. Multi-site, prospective clinical validation studies with demographically diverse populations are required before deployment in regulated clinical decision support contexts.
100: XI. CONCLUSION
101: This paper presented a complete deep learning pipeline for automated binary stress classification from single-lead ECG signals. The proposed CNN-BiLSTM + Attention architecture, augmented by transfer learning from MIT-BIH and DREAMER and trained on the WESAD benchmark, targets state-of-the-art performance metrics: accuracy ≥ 92%, sensitivity ≥ 94%, specificity ≥ 90%, and AUC-ROC ≥ 0.95. A rigorous five-stage preprocessing chain, four-strategy data augmentation, SMOTE class balancing, and a clinical person-independent evaluation protocol collectively position the system for real-world deployment. The Streamlit web application with attention weight visualization bridges the gap between model performance and clinical usability, enabling ECG stress inference without specialized programming expertise. The proposed system represents a meaningful step toward scalable, objective, wearable stress monitoring with broad applications in preventive cardiovascular care, workplace wellness, and remote patient monitoring.
